Chapter I

Origins of the Renaissance

The word Renaissance means "rebirth." Between the 14th and 16th centuries, Italian artists and scholars rediscovered the art, literature and philosophy of ancient Greece and Rome. Supported by wealthy patrons such as the Medici family, they placed the human being at the centre of their work, a movement known as Humanism.

c. 1305

Giotto Paints the Scrovegni Chapel

Giotto di Bondone breaks with flat Byzantine tradition, giving his figures weight, emotion and a sense of real space. He is often called the father of Western painting.

1401

The Baptistery Doors Competition

Florence holds a contest to design bronze doors for its Baptistery. Lorenzo Ghiberti wins; the event is often seen as the symbolic start of the Early Renaissance.

c. 1415

Brunelleschi and Linear Perspective

Architect Filippo Brunelleschi demonstrates a mathematical method for creating depth on a flat surface, transforming painting forever.

c. 1427

Masaccio's Holy Trinity

Masaccio applies perspective in a fresco at Santa Maria Novella, creating an illusion of architecture so convincing that viewers felt they could step inside it.

c. 1440

The Printing Press

Johannes Gutenberg's invention spreads classical texts and new ideas across Europe, carrying the Renaissance far beyond Italy.

Chapter II

The Great Masters

The High Renaissance, roughly 1490 to 1527, produced some of the most celebrated artists in history. Their mastery of anatomy, light and composition set a standard that artists would follow for centuries.

✎

Leonardo da Vinci

1452 – 1519

Painter, engineer, anatomist and inventor, Leonardo was the ultimate "Renaissance man." He perfected sfumato, a soft blending of tones that gives his figures a lifelike, mysterious quality.

Mona Lisa · The Last Supper · Vitruvian Man

Leonardo filled more than 7,000 pages of notebooks with studies of flight, water, anatomy and machines, all written in mirror script from right to left.

+ Did you know?
⚒

Michelangelo

1475 – 1564

A sculptor above all, Michelangelo carved heroic figures from single blocks of marble and spent four years painting the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel while lying on scaffolding.

David · Pietà · Sistine Chapel Ceiling

He considered himself a sculptor, not a painter. To carve David he took on a block of marble that other artists had abandoned as flawed.

+ Did you know?
✿

Raphael

1483 – 1520

Known for harmony, grace and clarity, Raphael blended the lessons of Leonardo and Michelangelo into serene compositions admired as the ideal of classical beauty.

The School of Athens · Sistine Madonna

Raphael ran one of the largest workshops in Rome and was chief architect of St Peter's Basilica. He died on his 37th birthday.

+ Did you know?
☀

Sandro Botticelli

c. 1445 – 1510

A favourite of the Medici court, Botticelli brought mythological subjects back to painting with flowing lines and poetic elegance.

The Birth of Venus · Primavera

Late in life Botticelli fell under the influence of the preacher Savonarola and is said to have burned some of his own secular paintings.

+ Did you know?

Chapter III

A Lasting Legacy

By the mid-16th century the Renaissance had spread to Flanders, Germany, France and Spain, inspiring artists such as Albrecht Dürer and Jan van Eyck. Its ideas still shape how we make and understand art today.

Realism and Perspective

Linear perspective, anatomical accuracy and the careful study of light became the foundation of academic art training for over four hundred years.

The Artist as Genius

Artists rose from anonymous craftsmen to celebrated individuals. Giorgio Vasari's Lives of the Artists (1550) became the first great work of art history.

Humanism and Science

The belief that people could understand the world through observation helped pave the way for the Scientific Revolution.

Mannerism and Beyond

Later artists stretched Renaissance rules into Mannerism, which in turn led to the drama of the Baroque, proof that the movement kept evolving.

"Every block of stone has a statue inside it, and it is the task of the sculptor to discover it." — Attributed to Michelangelo
